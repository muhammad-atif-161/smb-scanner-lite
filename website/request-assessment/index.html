What not to send

No passwords, no private keys, no customer data. A domain name, a company name and a description of the concern are enough to start the conversation.

The process

Four steps, and the first one is a human reading your request.

The restriction that makes the public demo safe is also why a private assessment is arranged rather than self-served.

01

You send the request

Company, domain, contact email, a short description of what you want to understand, and a confirmation that you own the domain or are authorised to request an assessment of it.

02

It is reviewed by hand

Nothing is scanned automatically. Requests are read, and domains are checked against the authorisation you describe before any work is agreed.

03

Scope and authorisation are agreed

Written authorisation, the controls in scope, the window in which the assessment runs, and the report format are confirmed in writing.

04

The assessment runs and you receive a report

Read-only external checks against the agreed scope, delivered as a report with the score, grade, findings, evidence and the consent record.

What an assessment covers

The full 22-control catalog: DNS integrity, email authentication, transport security, browser headers and network exposure. The public sandbox demonstrates eight of those controls on reserved domains; a private assessment covers the agreed scope for your infrastructure.

  • External and read-onlyNo credentials, no exploitation, no payloads, no internal access.
  • Evidence per findingEvery conclusive result quotes the observation that produced it.
  • Multiple formatsHTML for reading, JSON for tooling, CSV for tracking, TXT for tickets.
  • Consent documentedA hash-chained consent receipt is embedded in the report.

What it is not

An assessment is not a penetration test, not a compliance certification and not a guarantee that a business is secure. It reports what is observable from outside, with evidence, and states the limits of that view in the report itself.
