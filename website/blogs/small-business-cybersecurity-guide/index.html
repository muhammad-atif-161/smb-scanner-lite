A modest security baseline for a small company

Security improves faster when someone knows which decisions are theirs than when another dashboard is added.

Muhammad Atif Updated 4 November 2026 2 min read

Worth remembering

  • Protect critical accounts with MFA and reliable recovery.
  • Know which services and domains are actually yours.
  • Verify backups and public email policy separately.

A small company rarely has a full-time person for security. That does not mean it needs to begin with an expensive monitoring platform. Begin with ordinary operational controls that can be assigned to real people and verified without a new subscription.

First, protect the accounts that control everything else

List the administrator accounts for domain registration, DNS, email, cloud hosting, banking and backups. Require strong multi-factor authentication wherever supported, remove unused administrators and document secure recovery procedures. A compromised registrar account can undermine a perfectly configured domain.

Second, understand what is connected

Build a small inventory of employee devices, internet-facing services, third-party vendors, domains and important information. An old service with no owner tends to persist until something fails. You cannot patch or retire infrastructure nobody remembers.

Third, practice restoring the business

A backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis. Test recovery of a representative file and a critical service, then record how long the process took and who can perform it. Protect backup credentials independently from the primary environment.

Fourth, check the public domain

Review mail authentication policy and DNS service ownership. These are unusually visible from outside and lend themselves to repeatable observations. They do not establish your company's wider security posture, but they can identify manageable configuration work.

A monthly review that fits in a calendar

Check administrator access, overdue updates, backup recovery evidence and changes to key public DNS records. Assign actions, not just 'risk levels'. The discipline of reviewing what changed is more durable than a one-time score.
