SPF, DKIM and DMARC: three different jobs

The familiar three-letter acronyms are not three versions of one security control. Each answers a different question about a message.

Muhammad Atif Updated 4 November 2026 2 min read

Worth remembering

  • SPF authorizes envelope senders, not every visible From address.
  • DKIM signs a message using a selector-specific public key.
  • DMARC connects authentication to the domain the reader sees.

Someone receives an invoice that appears to be from your company. Their inbox displays your domain, but display alone says little about who sent it. Email authentication addresses that ambiguity with three separate mechanisms. Understanding how they connect is more useful than memorizing a checklist of record names.

SPF answers who may send

Sender Policy Framework publishes which mail infrastructure is authorized to send using a domain in the SMTP envelope. A receiving server evaluates the sending IP against the domain used by that envelope identity. The critical distinction is that the envelope sender may differ from the address the human reader sees in the From field.

An SPF record can also be malformed, duplicated, or too complicated to evaluate reliably. Check the complete policy, not just whether a DNS query returns a string beginning with v=spf1.

DKIM answers whether a signature verifies

DomainKeys Identified Mail adds a cryptographic signature to selected message headers and body content. A receiver retrieves a public key at a selector-specific DNS name and verifies the signature. A correctly published key is only part of the story: whether a particular outgoing message is signed can only be learned by inspecting that message.

There is no universal list of DKIM selectors. A scanner that checks several common selectors and finds none should report limited discovery, not assert that the organization never signs mail.

DMARC connects the pieces to the visible identity

DMARC evaluates whether SPF or DKIM passes with a domain aligned to the visible From address. It then communicates the publishing domain's policy, including monitoring (p=none) or requested enforcement (p=quarantine or p=reject). Receivers retain discretion, and real delivery also depends on reputation, content and local filtering.

A sensible first review

  1. List every service allowed to send on behalf of the domain.
  2. Query the domain's SPF and _dmarc TXT records.
  3. Send test messages through each legitimate platform and inspect authentication results.
  4. Correct missing signing or alignment before asking receivers to reject failed messages.
  5. Monitor DMARC reports when possible and adjust policy deliberately.
