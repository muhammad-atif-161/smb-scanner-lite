SPF, DKIM and DMARC, explained without the acronym soup

Three records, three jobs, one outcome: whether a receiving mail server has any reason to believe a message came from you. Here is the mental model that makes the rest of the internet's email advice make sense.

By Muhammad Atif Published 31 October 2026 5 min read

The short version

  • SPF says which servers may send mail for your domain. DKIM proves a message was not altered after it was signed. DMARC decides what a receiver does when either check fails.
  • SPF or DKIM on their own are plumbing. DMARC is the part that changes someone else's behaviour.
  • Alignment is the detail most people miss: DMARC asks whether the domain that authenticated matches the domain in the visible From line.
  • You can inspect all three on your own domain in about ten minutes, with three dig commands.

The original email protocol has no concept of a sender identity. Anyone who can reach a mail server can claim to be anyone, and for most of the internet's history the receiving side had nothing to check that claim against. SPF, DKIM and DMARC were added later, one problem at a time, by different working groups, which is precisely why they look like three unrelated things bolted onto the same system.

They are not unrelated. Think of them as three questions a receiving mail server asks:

  1. Is this machine allowed to send for that domain? (SPF)
  2. Was this message tampered with after the sender signed it? (DKIM)
  3. If either answer is bad, what should I do — and does the domain that passed even match the one the customer sees? (DMARC)

What each record actually does

The three mechanisms, in the order a receiving server usually evaluates them.
RecordWhere it livesWhat it answersWhat it cannot do
SPFA TXT record on the domain itselfWhich IP addresses or hosts are authorised to send mail using this domain as the envelope senderSurvive forwarding, or tell you anything about the visible From line
DKIMTXT records under <selector>._domainkeyWhether the message body and selected headers match what the sender signedProve the sender is trustworthy — only that the message was signed with a key that domain published
DMARCA TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.comWhat to do on failure, where to send reports, and whether authentication has to align with the From domainWork at all unless SPF or DKIM aligns for legitimate mail

The part that connects them: alignment

Here is the subtle bit that trips up almost everyone. SPF authenticates the envelope sender — a routing detail the recipient usually never sees. DKIM authenticates a domain that signed the message. The From line in the customer's inbox is a third thing entirely, and nothing forces them to agree.

So an attacker can send a message with your domain in the From line, use a domain they control for the envelope sender, pass SPF on their domain, and land in an inbox looking like you. DMARC closes that gap by asking for alignment: did the domain that passed SPF or DKIM match the domain in From? That question — not the pass itself — is what turns authentication into protection.

SPF passing for your domain is not the same as SPF passing for your domain on a message that claims to be from you.

What a healthy configuration looks like

Concretely, for a business on Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 with a couple of extra senders, a working setup looks like this. The exact mechanisms differ; the shape does not.

; the domain itself
yourdomain.com.        TXT  "v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all"

; the policy
_dmarc.yourdomain.com. TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com; adkim=r; aspf=r"

; one signing key (Google Workspace uses the "google" selector)
google._domainkey.yourdomain.com. TXT "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBg... "

Three things are worth noticing. The SPF record ends in -all, a hard fail, not ~all. The DMARC policy is p=reject, which means receivers are told to refuse failing mail outright. And the DKIM selector is named — selectors are not discoverable by design, so the only people who can check a selector are the ones who know what it is called.

A ten-minute check on your own domain

You do not need a tool for the basics. On macOS or Linux, three queries cover the three records. On Windows, the same questions work in any DNS lookup site or in PowerShell with Resolve-DnsName.

# 1. SPF (look for a single record, and for -all at the end)
dig +short TXT yourdomain.com

# 2. DMARC (look for v=DMARC1 and the p= value)
dig +short TXT _dmarc.yourdomain.com

# 3. DKIM (use the selector your provider documents, e.g. "google" or "selector1")
dig +short TXT google._domainkey.yourdomain.com

Read the answers for what they are, not for whether something replied. Two SPF records is a failure, not a merge. An SPF record that ends in ?all expresses no opinion at all. A DMARC record with p=none asks for reports and promises nothing. And a DKIM query that returns nothing may mean the selector name is wrong rather than the key being missing — which is exactly why "DKIM not found" from a checklist site deserves a second look before you panic.

Why "we already have SPF" is not the end of the conversation

SPF is the easiest of the three to publish and the least useful on its own. It breaks when mail is forwarded, it fails silently when a third-party tool sends on your behalf from an address you forgot to authorise, and it says nothing about whether the visible sender is you. A domain with SPF but no DMARC policy is a domain where anyone can still forge the From line without consequence.

The order that works is unglamorous: inventory every system that sends mail as your domain, give each one SPF authorisation and its own DKIM signature, then publish DMARC in monitoring mode, then enforce. Skipping to enforcement is how a business discovers, mid-quarter, that its invoices have been going to spam for a week.
