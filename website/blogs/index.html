Blog

Security writing for businesses without a security team

Practical guides to the parts of security you can actually change this week: email authentication, DNS records, TLS and browser headers, and how to judge an assessment report. Written for the person who also does the invoicing.

10 posts 5 clusters Updated 31 October 2026

Most security content is written either for engineers who already know the answer or for buyers who need to be frightened into one. This section is for the third reader: the owner or operations lead of a small business who has to decide what to fix, in what order, with no budget for a security team.

Every guide follows the same discipline. It explains the mechanism, shows the check you can run yourself, states what a good result looks like, and says plainly where the advice stops working. No invented statistics, no fear marketing, no "10 signs you are being hacked" listicles.

If you want the RFC-level version of a topic instead, the knowledge base holds the reference articles. The two sections link to each other on purpose, and neither repeats the other's job.

Blog or knowledge base?

Blog — a sequence to follow, with commands, checklists and a decision at the end.

Knowledge base — the reference: how a mechanism works, what it does not protect, and where the specification says so.

Both are written from the same method, and both link to the published methodology.

Editorial standard

What these guides will not do.

  • No invented evidenceNo fabricated breach statistics, customer stories or benchmark numbers. Where a figure comes from a published report, it is named.
  • No scanning by implicationNothing here encourages testing a domain you do not own. The instructions assume you are working on your own infrastructure.
  • No product-first answersThe mechanism is explained before the tool is mentioned, and the limit of the tool is stated in the same paragraph.
  • Corrections welcomeIf something is wrong or has aged badly, the contact form reaches the maintainer directly.

How often this is reviewed

Guides are reviewed when a specification, provider default or browser behaviour changes, and on a rolling annual basis otherwise. Each post shows its publication and last-updated date.

Posts that describe a provider's interface or a policy change carry a shorter review window, because those are the parts that go stale first.
