Your domain's DNS deserves an owner

DNS changes are small; the business effects can be enormous. The right first control is often knowing who can edit them.

Muhammad Atif Updated 4 November 2026 2 min read

Worth remembering

  • Inventory active records and the teams responsible.
  • Secure registrar and DNS-provider accounts before tuning optional records.
  • Use consistent external queries to review changes over time.

DNS directs visitors to websites, mail to providers and some trust decisions to public policy. It rarely receives the same attention as the applications behind it. That is odd: a record changed by mistake can interrupt an entire company before anyone notices.

Know who controls the zone

Document the registrar, authoritative DNS provider, account owners, recovery contacts and who may edit records. Enable strong authentication on those accounts. The most carefully written DNSSEC policy is not enough if an attacker can simply take over the control panel.

Build a purposeful record inventory

List the public records that support your domain: address records, mail routing, TXT policy, CAA and delegations. Add a business owner to each service. A hostname pointed at a retired platform is not inherently compromised, but it should be investigated and removed if unused.

Measure externally, cautiously

Compare answers from known resolvers and check authoritative data where possible. Resolver caches, geographic routing and provider defaults can explain legitimate differences. A timeout says the observation failed, not necessarily that the target misconfigured DNS.

Understand DNSSEC's narrow benefit

DNSSEC can help validating resolvers detect tampered DNS data through signatures and a chain of trust. It does not hide records, encrypt DNS traffic or prevent registrar compromise. An incorrectly maintained delegation may instead make valid names unreachable.

Give DNS a change log

For each edit, capture who approved it, what record changed, what result you expected and how you verified it externally. Repeat the same queries afterward. A reliable before/after record is more useful than a dashboard that only says 'healthy'.
